AI chatbots for law firms and accountancy practices: disclosure, no advice by the bot, confidentiality and conflict checks

Last reviewed 10 November 2026

Short answer. Say it is an AI in the first message. Keep the bot to intake, booking and general information about the firm; legal and tax advice stays with a qualified person who has checked the facts. Ask for names of the parties first and run the conflict check before the prospective client describes the matter, because lawyers must refuse a matter that risks a former client's confidences (CCBE Code 3.2.3). Treat the chatbot vendor as someone bound by your secrecy duty (2.3.4). Accountants, tax advisers and lawyers in certain transactions are obliged entities under the Anti-Money Laundering Directive, so the bot must never discuss due diligence outcomes or reports (Article 39). A firm's intake bot is not high-risk under Annex III point 8(a), which covers AI used by courts.

The AI disclosure

A firm's website chatbot interacts directly with members of the public, so Article 50(1) of the AI Act applies, and people must be told at the latest at the first interaction (Article 50(5)):

"Hi, I'm the AI assistant for Morgan & Field Solicitors. I can take your enquiry, explain how we work and book a call with one of our lawyers. I can't give legal advice."

That last sentence does two jobs: it tells people what the bot is, and it sets the limit the rest of this guide relies on. The disclosure wording guide has more options.

Information, not advice

There is no single EU rule on who may give legal advice; each Member State reserves different activities to lawyers, and tax advice is reserved in some countries and open in others. What is common is that the firm is responsible for what its chatbot says, and a confident wrong answer about a limitation period or a filing deadline is exactly the kind of statement a client relies on.

Keep the bot to:

  • what the firm does, who does it, fees and how to book;
  • general, non-personal information that the firm has written and checked (for example, "employment tribunal claims usually have short time limits, so contact us soon");
  • collecting the details a lawyer or accountant needs for the first call.

And out of:

  • applying the law to the user's facts ("you have a good case", "you don't need to file");
  • deadlines for the user's own matter;
  • anything that reads as a recommendation to act or not to act.

A model asked "should I sign this?" will try to help. Test that it declines and offers a call instead (see the pre-launch test script).

Conflict check before the facts

The CCBE Code of Conduct for European Lawyers, which many national codes follow, says a lawyer "may not advise, represent or act on behalf of two or more clients in the same matter if there is a conflict, or a significant risk of a conflict" (3.2.1), and "must also refrain from acting for a new client if there is a risk of breach of a confidence entrusted to the lawyer by a former client" (3.2.3).

A chatbot that invites a prospective client to describe the dispute in full, before anyone knows who the other side is, can leave the firm holding confidential information from someone it then cannot act for, sometimes the opponent of an existing client. A safer order for the intake flow:

  1. Name of the enquirer and the names of the other parties.
  2. Conflict check by the firm (in the practice management system, not by the model).
  3. Only then, a short description of the matter, ideally in a call or a secure form.

Tell the enquirer in the chat why you are asking for names first. For accountancy practices the logic is similar where the professional body's ethics code requires conflict checks, for example before acting for both sides of a business sale or a divorce settlement.

Confidentiality and the AI vendor

The CCBE Code says a lawyer "shall respect the confidentiality of all information that becomes known to the lawyer in the course of his or her professional activity" (2.3.2), that the duty "is not limited in time" (2.3.3), and that the lawyer must require "anyone engaged by him or her in the course of providing professional services to observe the same obligation" (2.3.4).

A chatbot provider that receives enquiry transcripts is engaged in providing the firm's services. In practice:

  • The contract binds the vendor to confidentiality and forbids using chats to train models.
  • The data processing agreement covers GDPR Article 28, and hosting is in the EU or a country with an adequacy decision or other Chapter V safeguard.
  • Retention is short for enquiries that never become clients.
  • Criminal law practices take extra care: data about criminal offences falls under GDPR Article 10.

Whether a chat with a firm's bot is covered by legal privilege depends on national law and is unclear in most Member States. Don't promise it in the bot.

Anti-money-laundering: keep it out of the chat

Under Article 2(1)(3) of the Anti-Money Laundering Directive (Directive (EU) 2015/849), obliged entities include "auditors, external accountants and tax advisors", and "notaries and other independent legal professionals" when they take part in listed transactions such as buying and selling property or businesses, managing client money, or creating companies and trusts.

Article 39(1) forbids obliged entities and their staff from disclosing to the customer "the fact that information is being, will be or has been transmitted" to the financial intelligence unit, or "that a money laundering or terrorist financing analysis is being, or may be, carried out".

A chatbot that answers "where is my onboarding?" from case data could breach that without anyone intending it. Keep customer due diligence status, source-of-funds questions and any internal review flags out of the data the bot can read, and give it a fixed answer for onboarding questions ("a member of the team will update you"). Article 39(6) allows a lawyer or accountant to try to dissuade a client from illegal activity; that is a conversation for a person, not the bot.

Regulation (EU) 2024/1624 replaces most of these rules from 10 July 2027, and keeps both the obliged-entity categories and the tipping-off ban.

Marketing through the bot

Article 24(1) of the Services Directive (Directive 2006/123/EC) required Member States to "remove all total prohibitions on commercial communications by the regulated professions", but Article 24(2) lets professional rules on independence, dignity, integrity and professional secrecy still apply to them. A sales-minded chatbot ("we win 95% of cases", "cheapest accountant in town") can break those rules in the same way as an advert making the same claims. Give the bot the same approved claims as the firm's website, and nothing more.

Legal research tools are a different question

Annex III point 8(a) of the AI Act lists as high-risk AI systems "intended to be used by a judicial authority or on their behalf to assist a judicial authority in researching and interpreting facts and the law and in applying the law to a concrete set of facts, or to be used in a similar way in alternative dispute resolution". A law firm's intake chatbot or its own research assistant is not used by or for a judicial authority, so point 8(a) does not cover it. A firm that runs an online dispute resolution service is in a different position and should check point 8(a) carefully.

Checklist

  • [ ] First message says it is an AI assistant and that it cannot give legal or tax advice.
  • [ ] The bot gives only general, checked information about the firm and the law, never advice on the user's facts or deadlines.
  • [ ] Intake asks for party names first; the conflict check runs before the matter is described.
  • [ ] The vendor contract covers confidentiality, no training on chats, GDPR Article 28 and hosting.
  • [ ] Short retention for enquiries that don't become clients; criminal data handled under GDPR Article 10.
  • [ ] No promise of privilege for chats.
  • [ ] Due diligence status, reports and review flags are not readable by the bot; onboarding questions get a fixed answer.
  • [ ] Marketing claims in the bot match approved website copy and the professional body's rules.
