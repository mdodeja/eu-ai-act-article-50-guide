AI chatbots for recruitment and job sites: disclosure, screening and the high-risk line

Last reviewed 8 November 2026

Short answer. Say it is an AI in the first message. A chatbot that only answers questions about jobs and the process is a normal chatbot. One that analyses and filters applications or evaluates candidates is a high-risk AI system under Annex III point 4(a), and the employer using it must tell candidates they are subject to it (Article 26(11)) and keep a human in charge. Inferring candidates' emotions from video or voice is prohibited (Article 5(1)(f)). Under the Pay Transparency Directive, candidates are entitled to the pay or pay range, and the bot must not ask about their current or previous pay.

Where the line is

What the bot does Status What follows
Answers questions about jobs, the company and the process Ordinary chatbot Article 50 disclosure
Books interviews, sends reminders Ordinary chatbot Article 50 disclosure; data minimisation
Asks knockout questions and rejects or ranks applicants High-risk, Annex III point 4(a) Deployer duties under Article 26, from 2 December 2027
Scores CVs or interview answers High-risk, Annex III point 4(a) Same
Reads emotions from video or voice Prohibited, Article 5(1)(f) Don't use it
Asks about current or past salary Pay Transparency Directive Article 5(2) Remove the question

The AI disclosure

A recruitment chatbot interacts directly with candidates, so Article 50(1) of the AI Act applies, and candidates must be told at the latest at the first interaction (Article 50(5)). Recruiting bots are often given a recruiter's name and photo, which is exactly the set-up that needs the label:

"Hi, I'm Northwind's AI recruiting assistant. I can answer questions about this role and the hiring process, and book your interview. Decisions about applications are made by our hiring team."

Only write the last sentence if it is true. The disclosure wording guide has more options.

When the bot becomes high-risk

Annex III point 4(a) of the AI Act lists as high-risk "AI systems intended to be used for the recruitment or selection of natural persons, in particular to place targeted job advertisements, to analyse and filter job applications, and to evaluate candidates". Point 4(b) covers systems used to make decisions on terms of work, promotion and termination, to allocate tasks based on behaviour or personal traits, and to monitor and evaluate workers.

A chatbot that asks screening questions and passes everything to a recruiter is a front end. A chatbot whose answers knock candidates out, or that produces a score or ranking the recruiter relies on, is analysing and filtering applications. That is the high-risk case, whether the scoring happens in the chat tool itself or in the applicant tracking system behind it. Under the Digital Omnibus, the high-risk duties for Annex III systems apply from 2 December 2027.

The provider (usually the software vendor) carries most of the high-risk duties. The employer or agency using the system is the deployer, and Article 26 gives it its own:

  • Tell candidates. Deployers of Annex III high-risk systems that make or assist decisions about people "shall inform the natural persons that they are subject to the use of the high-risk AI system" (Article 26(11)). This comes on top of the Article 50 chatbot disclosure.
  • Tell workers. For systems used at the workplace, such as internal mobility or performance tools, employers must inform workers' representatives and affected workers before putting the system into service (Article 26(7)).
  • Human oversight. Assign people with the competence and authority to oversee the system, and use it according to the provider's instructions (Article 26(1) and (2)).
  • Keep the logs the system generates, to the extent they are under the deployer's control (Article 26(6)).

Candidates can also have rights under the GDPR, including Article 22 on decisions based solely on automated processing with legal or similarly significant effects. Rejecting an application without any human involvement is the standard example.

No emotion reading in interviews

Article 5(1)(f) prohibits AI systems that infer the emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions, except for medical or safety reasons. It has applied since 2 February 2025. Video-interview tools that claim to measure enthusiasm, honesty or stress from facial expressions or tone of voice fall in this category. Turn such features off, and check the vendor's settings for defaults.

Pay ranges and pay history

The Pay Transparency Directive (Directive (EU) 2023/970) had a transposition deadline of 7 June 2026, and national laws are arriving at different speeds (reported by Mason Hayes & Curran and others; check the country). Article 5 gives two rules a recruiting bot has to follow:

  • Pay information. Applicants are entitled to information about the initial pay or its range for the position, for example in the job advert or before the interview (Article 5(1)). When a candidate asks the bot "what does this pay?", the answer should come from the vacancy data, not "it depends".
  • No pay history questions. The employer must not ask applicants about their pay history in current or previous jobs (Article 5(2)). Screening flows often include "What is your current salary?". Remove it, and make sure the model doesn't ask it on its own in a free-text conversation.

Article 5(3) also requires job vacancy notices and titles to be gender-neutral and the recruitment process to be non-discriminatory, which applies to job ads the bot writes too.

Candidates' data

Collect what the stage needs: contact details and the application to begin with, right-to-work documents only when an offer is near. Don't ask about health, family plans, religion or other sensitive topics in a screening chat; those are special category data under GDPR Article 9 and an obvious discrimination risk. Say in the privacy notice how long chat transcripts are kept, and whether the vendor trains on them.

Checklist

  • [ ] First message says it is an AI assistant, including on a named recruiter persona.
  • [ ] The firm has decided whether the bot only informs and books, or also filters and scores.
  • [ ] If it filters or scores: candidates are told they are subject to a high-risk AI system, and a person reviews the outcomes.
  • [ ] No candidate is rejected without human involvement.
  • [ ] Emotion analysis in video or voice interviews is off.
  • [ ] The bot gives the pay or pay range from the vacancy data.
  • [ ] No question about current or previous pay, in the fixed flow or from the model.
  • [ ] Job ads written by the bot are gender-neutral.
  • [ ] Screening asks for no sensitive data; transcript retention is in the privacy notice.
