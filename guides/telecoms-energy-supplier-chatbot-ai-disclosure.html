AI chatbots for telecoms providers and energy suppliers: disclosure, contract changes, switching and complaints

Last reviewed 11 November 2026

Short answer. Say it is an AI in the first message. If the bot sells a telecoms or energy contract, the customer gets a contract summary first (EECC Article 102(3); Electricity Directive Article 10(3)). When customers ask about a price rise or a change to their terms, the bot should tell them about their right to terminate, which is in both laws (EECC Article 105(4); Electricity Directive Article 10(4)). Termination procedures must not act as a disincentive to switching (EECC Article 105(1)), so a bot that loops retention offers is a risk. For telecoms, the receiving provider leads a switch (Article 106(6)). Complaints must be handled in a simple, fair and prompt manner (Electricity Directive Article 10(9)), so give the bot a clear route to a person.

What applies

Part of the chat Rule What to do
First message AI Act Article 50(1) and (5) Say it is an AI, at the latest at the first interaction
Bot sells a new contract EECC Article 102(3); Electricity Directive Article 10(3) Contract summary before the contract; no "done" in the chat without it
"Why has my bill gone up?" EECC Article 105(4); Electricity Directive Article 10(4) Answer from the actual notice, and state the right to terminate
"I want to cancel" EECC Article 105(1) One clear path; a retention offer at most once
"I want to switch" EECC Article 106; Electricity Directive Article 12 Explain who does what; don't block or delay
Complaints Electricity Directive Article 10(9); national telecoms rules Log it and hand it to a person

EECC is the European Electronic Communications Code, Directive (EU) 2018/1972. The Electricity Directive is Directive (EU) 2019/944. Both are directives, so the detail is in national law and the regulator's rules, which often go further.

The AI disclosure

A supplier's chat assistant talks directly to customers, so Article 50(1) of the AI Act applies, and customers must be told at the latest at the first interaction (Article 50(5)):

"Hi, I'm Northwind Energy's AI assistant. I can help with bills, meter readings, moving home and your tariff. Type 'agent' at any time to reach a person."

The disclosure wording guide has more options. Many of these chats start in an app or by SMS; the mobile app guide covers placement there.

Selling a contract: the summary comes first

For telecoms, Article 102(3) of the EECC requires providers to give consumers "a concise and easily readable contract summary", "free of charge to consumers, prior to the conclusion of the contract", including for distance contracts. If that is impossible for objective technical reasons, the summary is sent without undue delay afterwards and "the contract shall become effective when the consumer has confirmed his or her agreement".

For electricity, Article 10(3) of the Electricity Directive says final customers "shall be provided with a summary of the key contractual conditions in a prominent manner and in concise and simple language".

A chatbot that upgrades a handset plan or moves a customer to a new tariff in the chat is concluding a contract. The flow needs to:

  • generate and show the contract summary from the product system before the customer confirms;
  • for telecoms, if the summary can't be shown first, treat the contract as not yet effective until the customer confirms after receiving it;
  • never let the model paraphrase prices, commitment periods or exit fees from memory.

Price rises and contract changes: tell people they can leave

Both laws tie a change in terms to a right to walk away.

  • Telecoms. Under Article 105(4) of the EECC, end-users "shall have the right to terminate their contract without incurring any further costs" when the provider changes the contractual conditions, and providers must notify them "at least one month in advance". The right "shall be exercisable within one month after notification" (Member States can extend this by up to three months). Changes that are exclusively to the end-user's benefit, purely administrative with no negative effect, or required by law are excluded.
  • Electricity. Under Article 10(4) of the Electricity Directive, customers "shall be given adequate notice of any intention to modify contractual conditions and shall be informed about their right to terminate the contract when the notice is given", and they are free to dissolve the contract if they don't accept the new conditions.

Customers usually ask the bot when the notice arrives. A bot that explains the rise but leaves out the right to terminate gives a misleadingly incomplete answer at the moment it matters most. Give the bot the actual notice for that customer (date sent, what changed, the deadline to terminate) and a fixed sentence about the right to leave. If the bot can't see the notice, it should say so and hand over.

Cancelling: not a retention maze

Article 105(1) of the EECC requires that "conditions and procedures for contract termination do not act as a disincentive to changing service provider". Commitment periods for consumers can't be longer than 24 months.

A generative bot trained to "save" customers can turn a cancellation into several rounds of offers, questions and "are you sure?". Under 105(1) that is a risk, and national consumer rules on cancellation (some countries require an online cancellation button) may add more. A safer design:

  • recognise cancellation intent and confirm it once;
  • show the end date, any exit charge from the contract data, and what happens to the number;
  • make at most one retention offer, then complete the request or hand it to the team that will;
  • send written confirmation.

Switching: who does what

For telecoms, "the receiving provider shall lead the switching and porting processes" (EECC Article 106(6)). Loss of service during switching and number porting "shall not exceed one working day" (Article 106(5)). The losing provider's bot should not tell a customer they must cancel first and then port; it should explain that the new provider handles it.

For electricity, customers are entitled to switch "within a maximum of three weeks from the date of the request", and by 2026 the technical switching process must take "no longer than 24 hours" on any working day (Electricity Directive Article 12(1)). Article 12(2) says "at least household customers and small enterprises are not charged any switching-related fees". Exit charges on fixed-term contracts are a separate question that depends on national rules and the contract; quote the customer's own contract, not a general rule.

Complaints go to a person

Article 10(9) of the Electricity Directive gives final customers "the right to a good standard of service and complaint handling by their suppliers", and says suppliers "shall handle complaints in a simple, fair and prompt manner". Telecoms regulators set similar complaint-handling codes nationally.

A chatbot can take the details and give a reference number. It should not decide the complaint, argue with the customer, or close the chat as "resolved". Log the complaint in the complaints system, hand it to a person, and tell the customer the next step and timescale, including the out-of-court dispute body if the national rules require it.

Customers who say they can't pay, are on medical equipment, or are about to be disconnected need a person quickly. Treat those words as an immediate handover trigger.

Checklist

  • [ ] First message says it is an AI assistant and how to reach a person.
  • [ ] Any sale or plan change in the chat shows the contract summary before confirmation.
  • [ ] Prices, commitment periods and exit charges come from the product and contract systems, not the model.
  • [ ] Questions about a price rise or change are answered from the customer's actual notice, with the right to terminate stated.
  • [ ] Cancellation: one confirmation, at most one retention offer, then completion and written confirmation.
  • [ ] Switching answers say the receiving provider leads (telecoms) and give the real timescales.
  • [ ] Complaints are logged, handed to a person, and given a reference and next step.
  • [ ] Payment difficulty, medical need and disconnection trigger an immediate handover.
